## Synthesis
**1. Areas of agreement.** Both papers converge on the view that digital technologies are no longer peripheral to business activity but have become foundational to how work is organized, how consumers are reached, and how value is created. They share a diagnosis of disruption: Paper 1 emphasizes how the internet and social media have reshaped consumer behavior and marketing practice, while Paper 2 frames technology as a structural force transforming employment, organizational design, and the psychological contract between workers and firms. Both call for research that moves beyond description to examine mechanisms—how platforms alter decision-making, relationships, and power—rather than treating technology as a neutral tool. They also agree that the pace of change has outpaced scholarly understanding, creating a gap between rapid technological diffusion and the slower accumulation of rigorous evidence.

**2. Areas of disagreement.** The papers diverge in scope, disciplinary lens, and normative orientation. Paper 1 is largely optimistic and opportunity-focused, framing digital and social media marketing as a domain of strategic advantage, consumer engagement, and emerging research avenues for marketing scholars. Paper 2 is more cautionary and diagnostic, foregrounding risks such as job displacement, intensified surveillance, blurred work–life boundaries, and the psychological toll of constant connectivity. Paper 1 implicitly positions technology as a lever firms can wield for competitive gain, whereas Paper 2 positions it as an exogenous force reshaping labor markets in ways individuals and organizations must adapt to—often unevenly. They also differ in unit of analysis: Paper 1 centers the consumer–brand interface, while Paper 2 centers the employee–organization interface, leading to distinct theories of value and harm.
