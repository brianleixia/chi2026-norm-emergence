## Synthesis
Based on the limited excerpts provided, here is a synthesis of the two papers:

## 1. Points of Agreement

Both papers converge on the central premise that rapid technological advancement is fundamentally reshaping the modern landscape—one focused on marketing and consumer engagement, the other on work and organizational structures. They agree that digital and social technologies are not merely supplementary tools but transformative forces that have altered how businesses operate, how consumers and employees behave, and how value is created and exchanged. Both perspectives also imply an urgent need for scholarly research to keep pace with these changes, suggesting that existing theoretical frameworks may be inadequate for explaining contemporary realities. Additionally, the papers share an implicit assumption that organizations must adapt strategically to these technological shifts in order to remain competitive and relevant.

## 2. Points of Disagreement

While both papers examine technological disruption, their focal points and underlying logics differ in important ways. The marketing-focused paper centers on the **external-facing** implications of technology—how companies reach, influence, and engage consumers through digital channels—treating technology primarily as an enabler of market opportunities and customer relationships. In contrast, the organizational behavior paper examines **internal-facing** dynamics, focusing on how technology restructures work itself, employment relationships, and the lived experiences of workers. A deeper tension may also exist in their normative orientations: marketing scholarship tends to frame digital technology optimistically as an opportunity for growth and innovation, whereas organizational research often raises concerns about displacement, surveillance, dehumanization, and the erosion of worker autonomy.

## 3. Most Urgent Open Question

The most pressing open question emerging from these two perspectives is: **How can organizations harness digital and social technologies to create value for consumers and markets without simultaneously degrading the quality of work, worker wellbeing, and organizational health?** This question bridges the two literatures and remains unanswered because marketing research has largely treated employees as a means to customer-facing outcomes, while organizational research has often overlooked how customer-facing digital strategies reshape internal labor demands.
