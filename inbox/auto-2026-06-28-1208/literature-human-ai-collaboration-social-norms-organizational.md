## Synthesis
Based on the snippets provided, I can offer only limited synthesis, as the excerpts are truncated. Here's what can be inferred:

**1. Points of Agreement**

Both papers acknowledge that digital technology is fundamentally reshaping core domains of economic and organizational life. Paper 1 emphasizes how the internet and social media have transformed consumer behavior and business practices, creating significant opportunities for organizations that adapt their marketing strategies. Paper 2 similarly recognizes that rapid technological advances are changing the nature of work and employment, making these questions central concerns for organizational psychology and management scholarship. Together, they agree that digital transformation is not a peripheral trend but a structural shift requiring new theoretical and practical frameworks.

**2. Points of Disagreement**

A key divergence lies in their **analytical orientation toward technology's effects**. Paper 1 adopts a largely **opportunity-focused lens**, framing social and digital marketing as offering "significant opportunities" for organizations to engage consumers in novel ways. Paper 2, by contrast, appears to approach technology's workplace implications with greater **ambivalence and scrutiny**, treating its effects on work and organizations as an open question requiring critical investigation rather than as a straightforwardly beneficial force. This reflects a broader tension in the literature between techno-optimistic accounts of digital tools and more cautious assessments of their disruptive consequences.

**3. Most Urgent Open Question**

The most pressing unresolved question is: **Under what conditions do digital and social technologies generate value for organizations and workers versus producing harms such as worker displacement, eroded autonomy, or consumer manipulation?** Both papers gesture at this question but neither resolves it. Addressing it requires longitudinal research tracing whether the "opportunities" highlighted in Paper 1 materialize equitably across stakeholders, or whether the disruptive workplace changes examined in Paper 2 compound inequalities that organizations and policymakers have not yet learned to govern.
