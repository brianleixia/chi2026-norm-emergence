# Research Synthesis: Multi-Agent Security & Agentic AI Governance

## 1. Points of Agreement

Both papers converge on the recognition that **multi-agent systems have outgrown traditional security and governance paradigms**. They share a core concern that as AI agents—particularly those built on large language models—begin to interact with each other, with humans, and across digital and physical environments, existing frameworks (whether cybersecurity, AI safety, or classical control theory) are insufficient to address the resulting emergent risks. Both works emphasize that the *interactions between agents* generate novel vulnerabilities and coordination problems that cannot be reduced to the behavior of any single agent in isolation. Additionally, they implicitly agree on the need for **adaptive, systems-level approaches** rather than static, component-level fixes, suggesting that governance mechanisms must evolve alongside the agents they oversee.

## 2. Points of Disagreement

The papers diverge in their **analytical focus and proposed solutions**. The first paper frames the problem primarily through the lens of *security*—emphasizing adversarial threats, attack surfaces, and defense in interconnected agent ecosystems, treating other agents as potential sources of compromise. The second paper, by contrast, adopts a *governance and control-theory* perspective, treating agent interactions as a coordination problem to be managed through information flow, adaptive interfaces, and principled system design. This leads to a subtle but important tension: the first paper implicitly assumes a **threat-oriented worldview** where agents may be malicious or compromised, while the second assumes a more **cooperative worldview** where agents have aligned goals but may behave unpredictably. Their methodological commitments also differ—one leans on adversarial analysis and red-teaming, the other on formal control architectures and interface design.

## 3. Most Urgent Open Question

The most pressing unresolved question is: **How can we design governance and security mechanisms that simultaneously defend against adversarial multi-agent threats while remaining flexible enough to accommodate the cooperative, adaptive coordination that beneficial agentic systems require?** Neither paper fully reconciles these
