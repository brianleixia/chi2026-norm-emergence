# Research Synthesis

## 1. Points of Agreement

Both papers converge on the recognition that conventional security and governance models—designed for isolated software systems or human-only organizations—fail when AI agents become first-class actors in complex, multi-actor environments. The first paper frames this as a security challenge, noting that interacting AI systems introduce attack surfaces and adversarial dynamics that traditional cybersecurity frameworks (focused on perimeter defense, static trust, and single-system integrity) cannot address. The second paper arrives at a similar conclusion from a governance angle, arguing that as agentic AI participates in sociotechnical systems, accountability, coordination, and decision authority become distributed in ways that existing institutional and engineering governance structures are not equipped to handle. Both also implicitly agree on the urgency of moving from reactive or post-hoc controls toward designs that embed security and governance considerations into the architecture of multi-agent systems from the outset, rather than retrofitting them.

## 2. Points of Disagreement

The papers diverge most clearly in their primary concern and methodological orientation. The first adopts a *threat-centric* posture, treating the central problem as adversarial interference—where one agent, malicious or compromised, can manipulate, deceive, or corrupt other agents or the shared environment. Its vocabulary is that of attack surfaces, trust boundaries, and resilience against intelligent adversaries. The second, by contrast, is oriented around *coordination and legitimacy*—asking how authority, accountability, and information flow should be structured among humans, AI agents, and institutions so that the system as a whole functions as intended. It is less concerned with a malicious insider than with the ordinary problem of ensuring that an AI acting with significant autonomy remains answerable to human values, oversight bodies, and legal regimes. A subtler tension lies in scope: the security paper tends to treat interactions as technical protocols between agents, while the governance paper insists these interactions are inseparable from the social, organizational, and institutional contexts in which agents are deployed.
