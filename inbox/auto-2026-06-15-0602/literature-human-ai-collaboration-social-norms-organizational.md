## Synthesis
## 1. Points of Agreement

Both papers converge on the view that digital technologies are not merely incremental tools but **transformative forces reshaping core human and organizational activities**. The marketing paper emphasizes that the internet and social media have fundamentally altered consumer behavior and business practices, creating both opportunities and challenges for organizations adapting to digital channels. Similarly, the technology-and-work paper frames technological advancement as a disruptive force that compels organizational psychology scholars to rethink how work is structured and experienced. Both implicitly endorse the idea that technological change outpaces our theoretical frameworks, requiring scholars to continuously update concepts to remain relevant.

## 2. Points of Disagreement

The papers diverge primarily in **scope and level of analysis**. The marketing paper is micro-applied, focusing on how digital tools shift specific marketing functions, consumer-firm interactions, and competitive strategy within relatively bounded industry contexts. The work-and-organizations paper, by contrast, operates at a macro-structural level, examining technology's broad psychological, social, and ethical consequences for employment, labor markets, and organizational design. A further tension lies in **orientation**: the marketing literature tends to frame technology as an enabler of opportunity (new channels, targeting capabilities, engagement), whereas the work-and-organizations literature tends to foreground risk and disruption (job displacement, surveillance, worker well-being, power asymmetries).

## 3. Most Urgent Open Question

The most pressing unresolved question is: **How should interdisciplinary frameworks be built to capture the bidirectional spillovers between digital marketing ecosystems and the changing nature of work?** Marketing-driven automation, algorithmic personalization, and platform-mediated labor (e.g., influencers, gig workers, AI-augmented service roles) simultaneously transform consumer-facing practices and workforce structures, yet these literatures rarely inform one another. Bridging this gap is essential for understanding who benefits, who bears the costs, and how regulatory and ethical guardrails should be designed.
