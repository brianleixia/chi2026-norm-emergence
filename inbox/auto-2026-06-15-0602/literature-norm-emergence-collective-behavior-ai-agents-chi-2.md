# Research Synthesis: Multi-Agent AI Security & Governance

## 1. Points of Agreement

Both papers converge on the recognition that traditional single-agent AI safety and cybersecurity paradigms are insufficient for the emerging reality of multi-agent systems. They share a foundational concern that as AI agents become increasingly autonomous and capable of direct interaction—whether with other AI agents or humans in sociotechnical environments—the attack surface expands dramatically beyond conventional threat models. Both works emphasize that security in multi-agent settings is fundamentally a system-level property, not merely the sum of individual agent safety properties. Additionally, both papers implicitly acknowledge the critical role of information flow control and structured coordination mechanisms in managing risk: the first through its focus on securing agent-to-agent interactions, and the second through its adaptive information contracts governing agentic AI behavior.

## 2. Points of Divergence

The papers differ significantly in their primary framing and methodological orientation. The first paper adopts a **security-centric lens**, treating the problem as one of adversarial threats, vulnerabilities, and defenses across communicating AI agents—analogous to extending network security into the agent domain. The second paper takes a **systems engineering and governance lens**, framing agentic AI integration as a sociotechnical design challenge requiring adaptive contracts and organizational coordination between human and AI stakeholders. Consequently, the first emphasizes *threat models and countermeasures*, while the second emphasizes *design principles and governance mechanisms*. The first implicitly assumes adversarial conditions as the primary concern, whereas the second treats cooperation, alignment, and dynamic adjustment as central—reflecting different assumptions about whether multi-agent interaction is primarily a conflict or coordination problem.

## 3. Most Urgent Open Question

**How can we formally unify adversarial threat modeling with cooperative governance frameworks?** Neither paper adequately bridges the gap between defending against malicious or misaligned agents (the security perspective) and designing systems where agents cooperate under adaptive rules (the governance perspective).
