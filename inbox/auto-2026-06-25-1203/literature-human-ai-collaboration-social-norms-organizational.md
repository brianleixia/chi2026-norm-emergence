## Synthesis
**What they agree on**
Both papers share a core conviction that digital technologies are not merely supplementary tools but fundamental forces reshaping the landscape in which people and institutions operate. They concur that the rapid pace of technological change has outstripped organizations' ability to adapt, creating a persistent gap between technological capability and organizational/strategic response. Both emphasize that the internet, social platforms, and related technologies have fundamentally altered human behavior—whether as consumers or as workers—and both call on their respective fields (marketing scholarship and organizational psychology/behavior) to develop more agile, evidence-based research agendas. They also implicitly agree that the consequences of these shifts are uneven, affecting individuals, organizations, and societies in complex ways that resist simple narratives.

**Where they disagree**
The papers diverge most clearly in their **locus of analysis and assumed beneficiary** of digital transformation. The marketing paper (Setting the future of digital and social media marketing research) frames technology largely as an **opportunity**—a domain where firms can deepen consumer engagement, refine targeting, and build competitive advantage. The work-and-organizations paper (How Technology Is Changing Work and Organizations), by contrast, adopts a more **cautious and ambivalent stance**, foregrounding how technology disrupts job security, skill demands, autonomy, and worker well-being, with outcomes that are often ambiguous or negative for employees. Additionally, the marketing paper tends to treat consumer agency as enhanced and empowered through digital channels, while the organizations paper suggests that worker agency is frequently diminished through algorithmic control, surveillance, and deskilling.
