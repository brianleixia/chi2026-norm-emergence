## Synthesis
# Research Synthesis: Multi-Agent Security and Agentic AI Governance

**1. Points of Agreement**

Both papers converge on the recognition that traditional security and governance frameworks are insufficient for environments populated by interacting, semi-autonomous AI agents. The first paper explicitly argues that "security challenges beyond traditional cybersecurity and AI safety frameworks" are emerging as AI agents interact directly across digital and physical environments, while the second paper echoes this by emphasizing that "complex sociotechnical environments" with multiple agents—including humans and LLM-powered agentic AI—demand new integrated approaches. Both also implicitly accept that governance cannot be retrofitted as an afterthought; it must be embedded into system design from the outset. Each paper treats multi-agent interaction itself—rather than the individual agent—as the primary unit of risk and analysis, and both implicitly endorse adaptive, rather than static, regulatory or technical safeguards in the face of evolving agent capabilities.

**2. Points of Disagreement**

The two papers diverge meaningfully in focus and normative ambition. The security-focused paper concentrates on adversarial threats, trust between agents, and the technical attack surface created when agents negotiate and act on each other's behalf—framing the problem largely as one of *preventing harm*. The governance-focused paper, by contrast, centers on adaptive information-flow controls and integrated system design, framing the problem as one of *enabling coordinated behavior* among humans, organizations, and AI agents under legitimate institutional authority. The security paper tends to treat governance as a secondary concern subordinate to threat mitigation, whereas the governance paper treats security as one constraint among many within a broader sociotechnical design problem. Additionally, the first paper foregrounds emergent risks from agent-to-agent interaction across untrusted environments, while the second assumes a more bounded organizational context where formal governance policies can be authored and enforced.
