## Synthesis
# 1. What do the papers agree on?

Both papers share a clear consensus that digital and technological transformation represents one of the most consequential shifts of our era, fundamentally reshaping human behavior, organizational structures, and professional practices. The marketing paper emphasizes that the internet and social media have irrevocably altered consumer behavior and the way companies operate, while the organizational psychology paper stresses that rapid technological advances are changing the very nature of work and employment. Both adopt a forward-looking stance, arguing that traditional frameworks are insufficient for understanding these new realities. Each paper calls for renewed scholarly attention to guide both practice and policy, implicitly treating the current pace of change as too rapid for existing theory to adequately explain or predict.

# 2. Where do they disagree?

The papers differ primarily in their unit of analysis, disciplinary lens, and normative posture. The marketing paper focuses on the consumer–firm interface, treating technology as an opportunity-laden tool for engagement, value creation, and competitive advantage — a relatively optimistic framing oriented toward strategic application. The organizational psychology paper, by contrast, zooms in on the worker and the employment relationship, foregrounding questions of job displacement, skill demands, autonomy, and well-being, often with a more cautious or critical tone. In short, the marketing view tends to see digital technology as a *means* of doing business better, whereas the organizational psychology view treats it as a *force* reshaping labor and human experience, with ambiguous and uneven consequences for individuals.

# 3. Most urgent open question?

The most urgent open question is: **How can organizations harness the opportunities of digital and social media marketing *without* exacerbating the harms to workers, consumers, and society that rapid technological change is producing?** Bridging these two literatures is essential, because research on the strategic benefits of digital engagement has largely proceeded in isolation from research on its human and organizational costs. Future scholarship needs integrative frameworks that link customer-facing digital strategy to internal workforce implications, ethical governance, and long-term social outcomes.
