## Synthesis
**1. What do the papers agree on?**

Both papers converge on the view that digital and technological transformation is reshaping core organizational and consumer-facing domains. The first paper focuses on social and digital marketing, emphasizing that internet and social media adoption have fundamentally altered consumer behavior and how companies conduct business. The second paper addresses how technology is changing work and organizations more broadly, noting that rapid advances in technology have made the question of its impact on work and employment highly salient for organizational scholars. Both share an underlying assumption that these shifts are not peripheral but structurally significant—warranting serious scholarly attention, new theoretical lenses, and revised research agendas to keep pace with ongoing change.

**2. Where do the papers disagree?**

The papers diverge primarily in their analytical focus and the locus of change they examine. The marketing paper centers on the external-facing domain—how firms communicate with, influence, and extract value from consumers in digital environments—treating social media as a marketplace phenomenon driven by consumer behavior shifts. The technology and work paper, by contrast, looks inward at the employment relationship and organizational structure, focusing on how technology reshapes job design, worker psychology, and organizational functioning. Implicitly, the two may also differ on the perceived valence of change: the marketing framing tends to emphasize "significant opportunities" for organizations, while the work-and-organizations framing is more circumspect, framing technological change as a pressing question with unresolved consequences for workers and institutions.

**3. What is the most urgent open question?**

The most urgent open question is how the convergence of these two trajectories—digital transformation of consumer-facing functions and technological transformation of internal work—should be studied jointly rather than in silos. Existing research treats marketing evolution and organizational change as parallel streams, yet firms increasingly experience them simultaneously: algorithms that personalize marketing also surveil and shape worker performance, and platforms built for consumer engagement simultaneously restructure labor and employment.
