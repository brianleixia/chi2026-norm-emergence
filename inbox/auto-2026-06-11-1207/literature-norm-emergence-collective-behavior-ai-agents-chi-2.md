## Synthesis
# Synthesis of Multi-Agent AI Security and Agentic AI Governance

## Points of Agreement

Both papers recognize that multi-agent and agentic AI systems introduce fundamentally novel security and governance challenges that extend beyond conventional cybersecurity and AI safety paradigms. They converge on the observation that traditional frameworks—designed for either isolated AI systems or static human-institutional structures—are inadequate for environments where autonomous agents interact, negotiate, and make decisions in dynamic sociotechnical settings. Each paper emphasizes the need for cross-disciplinary approaches, drawing from distributed systems, information theory, game theory, and governance theory to address emerging risks. Both also acknowledge that as AI agents become more capable and autonomous, the attack surface and decision-space of these systems grow in ways that demand new theoretical and practical tools for ensuring security, reliability, and alignment with human values.

## Points of Divergence

The two papers differ in their primary lens and proposed solutions. The Open Challenges paper is primarily **security-focused**, treating interacting AI agents as a new threat domain and emphasizing risks such as adversarial manipulation, emergent unsafe behaviors, and the difficulty of securing systems where no single entity controls the entire interaction. It frames the problem as one of "systems of interacting AI agents" and stresses the need for defensive mechanisms, threat modeling, and secure interaction protocols. In contrast, the Integrated Design paper is **governance- and design-oriented**, proposing "Adaptive Information" as an integrative framework that co-designs agentic AI systems with their governance structures from the outset. Rather than treating security as an add-on, it advocates embedding adaptive governance mechanisms directly into system architecture, focusing on how information flows between human and AI agents can be structured to maintain accountability, transparency, and control. The first paper asks "how do we protect against bad outcomes in multi-agent settings?" while the second asks "how do we architect systems and governance together to produce good outcomes?"
