## Synthesis
Based on the two paper excerpts you've provided, here is a synthesis (though I should note both excerpts are truncated, so my analysis is necessarily limited):

## 1. Points of Agreement

Both papers converge on the view that digital technologies—social media in one case, AI in the other—are fundamentally reshaping organizational practice and consumer/business relationships. Each frames technology as a transformative force that compels a rethinking of established marketing and management theory. Both also implicitly recognize that these technologies are not merely tools but catalysts for structural change, requiring researchers to update frameworks, vocabularies, and research agendas. The first paper emphasizes the consumer-behavior and firm-strategy implications of digital and social media, while the second focuses on AI's managerial implications, yet both treat technological disruption as a phenomenon demanding scholarly attention.

## 2. Points of Disagreement or Divergent Emphasis

The papers differ in their locus of analysis and underlying posture toward technology. The marketing paper (1) is primarily *descriptive and agenda-setting*, charting how digital and social media alter consumer behavior and offering significant opportunities to organizations—suggesting a largely optimistic, opportunity-oriented framing. The AI/management paper (2) adopts a more *critical and dialectical* stance, explicitly naming the "automation–augmentation paradox," which acknowledges that AI simultaneously displaces and enhances human managerial work. Where Paper 1 treats technology as an enabler of new research opportunities, Paper 2 highlights tension and tradeoffs. Additionally, Paper 1 centers the *firm–consumer* interface, whereas Paper 2 centers the *human–machine* interface within organizations.

## 3. Most Urgent Open Question

The most pressing unresolved question bridging both papers is: **Under what conditions does digital and AI technology augment human capability in marketing and management, and when does it merely automate—and ultimately deskill—the human roles it transforms?** Without a clearer theoretical and empirical demarcation between augmentation and automation in practice, both fields risk producing celebratory research that overstates benefits
