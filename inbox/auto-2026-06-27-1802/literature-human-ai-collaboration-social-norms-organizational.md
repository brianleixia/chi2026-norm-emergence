## Synthesis
**1. Points of Agreement**

Both papers converge on the central premise that digital technology represents a fundamental, transformative force reshaping core domains of economic and organizational life. Paper 1 emphasizes how the internet and social media have altered consumer behavior and forced companies to rethink marketing strategy, while Paper 2 examines how technology more broadly is restructuring work itself and the nature of organizations. They share a forward-looking orientation, both arguing that scholarship must move beyond documenting incremental changes to anticipate the structural shifts ahead. The authors in both pieces implicitly agree that technological transformation is not merely a technical matter but a deeply human and organizational one, demanding interdisciplinary inquiry that integrates behavioral, strategic, and structural perspectives.

**2. Points of Disagreement**

The two papers diverge in their focal concern and implicit normative stance. Paper 1 frames the digital shift as primarily an *opportunity* for marketers and consumers, foregrounding how firms can leverage social and digital tools to create value, build relationships, and respond to evolving consumer behavior. Its tone is largely strategic and optimistic. Paper 2, by contrast, approaches technology's organizational impact with greater caution and analytic distance, emphasizing disruption, uncertainty, and the need for scholars to understand consequences that may not yet be fully visible. Where Paper 1 centers on the interface between firms and consumers, Paper 2 is more concerned with the internal dynamics of work, employment, and organizational design, reflecting a broader view of what is at stake.

**3. Most Urgent Open Question**

The most pressing question emerging from these two papers together is: **How can scholars and practitioners simultaneously harness the strategic opportunities of digital technologies while anticipating and mitigating their disruptive effects on work, workers, and organizational structures?** In other words, how do we build an integrated research agenda that connects the *consumer-facing* transformation studied in marketing scholarship with the *labor- and organization-facing* transformation studied in organizational research? Without bridging these two streams, our understanding of the digital economy will remain fragmented, and we will be poorly equipped
