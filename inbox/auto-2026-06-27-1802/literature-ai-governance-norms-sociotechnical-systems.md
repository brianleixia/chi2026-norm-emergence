**1. What they agree on:** With no papers provided, there is no empirical or theoretical ground for any claim of agreement. However, were a set of papers to exist, agreement among them would constitute the established consensus of the field — the propositions treated as settled, undisputed, or no longer requiring defense. Synthesizing agreement means identifying the convergent core: the shared vocabulary, the methods no one questions, the findings that have become background assumptions. The act of naming agreement is itself a rhetorical move, because what counts as "agreed upon" depends on which voices are included in the synthesis. A useful synthesis always asks: *Who is not represented in this apparent consensus, and what does that absence conceal?*

**2. Where they disagree:** Genuine disagreement in a literature is rarer than it appears, because most published disagreement is conducted within a shared frame — a shared ontology, a shared set of acceptable methods, a shared definition of what would count as evidence. Real conflict emerges only at the boundaries of that frame, where one camp's foundational assumption is another camp's unexamined premise. A strong synthesis of disagreement distinguishes surface-level disputes (different conclusions drawn from the same data or method) from deep structural disputes (different conceptions of what the field is *for*). The most productive disagreements are almost always meta-disagreements about scope, definition, or evidence rather than direct contradictions of fact.
