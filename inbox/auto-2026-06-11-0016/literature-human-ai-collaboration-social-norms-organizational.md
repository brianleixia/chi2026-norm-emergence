## Synthesis
## 1. Where the Papers Agree

Both papers converge on a foundational premise: digital and technological transformation is fundamentally reshaping core structures of contemporary life—whether consumer behavior and marketing practices (Paper 1) or work, employment, and organizational functioning (Paper 2). They share an assumption that the pace of technological change has outstripped existing theoretical frameworks, leaving scholars in need of new conceptual tools and research agendas. Both emphasize that technology is not merely a tool but a relational force that reshapes human behavior, organizational processes, and power dynamics. Additionally, both implicitly call for interdisciplinary perspectives, acknowledging that understanding these shifts requires moving beyond traditional disciplinary silos toward more integrative, future-oriented research programs.

## 2. Where the Papers Disagree (or Differ in Emphasis)

The papers diverge in their unit of analysis and primary concern. Paper 1 situates the discussion at the **marketplace boundary**, focusing on consumer–firm interactions, branding, and the strategic implications of social and digital channels for marketing scholarship. Its lens is outward-facing, concerned with how organizations reach and influence external audiences. Paper 2, by contrast, zooms into the **internal organization**, examining how technology restructures jobs, roles, identities, and employment relationships. It is concerned with the worker, the workplace, and the psychological contract. Consequently, Paper 1 tends to frame technology as opportunity-laden (new channels, engagement, value creation), whereas Paper 2 adopts a more ambivalent tone, treating technology as a disruptive force with uneven benefits and costs—including surveillance, precarity, and skill displacement.

## 3. Most Urgent Open Question

The most pressing question bridging both papers is: **How can scholarship theorize the bidirectional, recursive relationship between external digital consumer environments and internal organizational redesign?** In other words, as social and digital platforms reshape consumer expectations and behaviors *outside* the firm, those changes simultaneously demand new internal structures, roles, and competencies *inside* the firm—and vice versa.
