## Synthesis
# Research Synthesis

## 1. Points of Agreement

Both papers converge on the recognition that digital technology has fundamentally restructured core social and organizational processes, demanding new theoretical frameworks to keep pace. They agree that the internet and digital platforms have shifted consumer behavior, business operations, and the nature of work itself in ways that traditional models cannot adequately explain. Both call for scholarly research to move beyond descriptive accounts and toward more nuanced understanding of how actors (consumers, employees, organizations) adapt to and co-construct these technological environments. They also share an implicit acknowledgment that technology is not a neutral tool but a transformative force that reshapes relationships, identities, and institutional logics.

## 2. Points of Disagreement

The papers differ in scope, level of analysis, and disciplinary orientation. The first focuses specifically on marketing and consumer-facing contexts, emphasizing opportunities for firms to engage customers through digital channels and the need for marketing research to address evolving consumer behavior. The second zooms out to organizational psychology and the workplace, foregrounding employment relationships, labor dynamics, and the psychological implications of technological change. Consequently, the first adopts an opportunity-oriented, value-creation framing, while the second tends toward a more critical or cautionary stance on issues such as job displacement, worker autonomy, and organizational control. They also differ in their unit of analysis—one centers the consumer-firm dyad, the other the worker-organization relationship.

## 3. Most Urgent Open Question

The most pressing open question is: **How can research frameworks integrate the consumer-side and employee-side perspectives of digital transformation into a unified understanding of socio-technical change?** As digital platforms increasingly blur boundaries between work, consumption, and identity (e.g., gig economies, creator cultures, algorithmic management of both customers and workers), siloed disciplinary approaches risk missing cross-cutting dynamics. A research agenda that bridges marketing and organizational scholarship could illuminate how the same technologies simultaneously reconfigure market relationships and employment relations, producing more holistic insights into the future of digital society.
