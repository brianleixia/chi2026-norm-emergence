**1. What do they agree on?**

Across the literature, researchers consistently find that heavy and passive use of social media is associated with higher rates of depressive symptoms, anxiety, and body-image dissatisfaction among adolescents. They also agree that the relationship is correlational rather than purely causal, that individual factors such as pre-existing mental health vulnerabilities, sleep quality, and offline social support moderate these effects, and that girls and older adolescents tend to show stronger negative associations than boys and younger users. There is broad consensus that social media is neither uniformly harmful nor uniformly beneficial—its impact depends on *how* it is used (passive scrolling vs. active engagement) and *what* content is encountered.

**2. Where do they disagree?**

The most contested question is the *magnitude* and *direction* of causality. Some longitudinal studies argue that social media use is a meaningful driver of declining mental health, accounting for a measurable share of the rise in adolescent depression since the early 2010s, while others—often drawing on large-scale or natural-experiment designs—contend that the causal effect is small, overstated, or even negligible once selection effects and reverse causation are accounted for. A second disagreement concerns the mechanisms: some researchers emphasize social comparison and FOMO as primary drivers, others highlight disrupted sleep, displacement of in-person interaction, or algorithmic amplification of harmful content. There is also unresolved tension over whether "addictive" or "compulsive" use is a useful clinical construct or a moralizing label that obscures more mundane explanations.
